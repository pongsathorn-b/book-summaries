# Essentialism — Greg McKeown

## Overview

Greg McKeown's core argument: the way most people work is broken. They're diffused, reactive, and spread too thin. The alternative is essentialism — the disciplined pursuit of less. Not getting more done in less time, but getting only the right things done. Essentialism is not about time management or productivity hacks — it's about making the few critical decisions that really matter.

## Core Concepts

### The Essentialist Mindset
Non-essentialists ask "how can I do it all?" Essentialists ask "which problems am I uniquely qualified to solve?" The shift is from "I can do anything" to "I will do only what matters." This is not minimalism for its own sake — it's the ruthless elimination of everything that isn't essential.

### The Explore/Eliminate/Execute Framework
Essentialism has three phases:
- **Explore**: Invest time to identify the essential — evaluate many options before committing.
- **Eliminate**: Say no clearly and without guilt — protect your time from non-essential demands.
- **Execute**: Remove obstacles and create a system that makes the essential the path of least resistance.

### The Mindful YES
Every yes is a no to something else. Before saying yes to any request, ask: "Is this the most meaningful use of my time?" A mindful yes is said slowly. A quick yes is almost always the wrong answer — it's usually said to avoid social discomfort, not to serve your priorities.

### The 90% Rule
Rate opportunities from 0-100. If it's below 90, automatically reject it. The 90% rule forces you to be ruthless — most "good" opportunities are actually "mediocre" in disguise. This sounds harsh, but it creates space for the truly essential.

### Play and Sleep
Play is not a luxury — it's a biological necessity for cognitive function, creativity, and decision-making. Sleep is not laziness — it's when the brain consolidates learning and restores energy. Non-essentialists sacrifice both in the name of "productivity." Essentialists protect them fiercely.

### Buffer and Bulletproofing
Add 50% buffer to all time estimates — projects always take longer than expected. Bulletproof your essential commitments by identifying the three most likely points of failure and addressing them before they occur.

## Key Lessons

1. The pursuit of less is more powerful than the pursuit of more.
2. Every yes is a no to something else — say yes slowly and deliberately.
3. The 90% rule: if it's not a clear yes, it's a no.
4. Explore before committing — invest time in identifying the essential.
5. Protect play and sleep — they're not luxuries, they're requirements.
6. Add 50% buffer to all time estimates — projects always take longer.
7. Remove obstacles to the essential — make doing the right thing the path of least resistance.
8. Make non-essential apologies: "I can't because I'm focusing on something important."

## Practical Applications

- Use the 90% rule on your next opportunity — reject everything below 90.
- Practice the mindful yes: sleep on any request before accepting.
- Create an "essential list" of 3 priorities — everything else is secondary.
- Add 50% buffer to all project timelines.
- Protect sleep and play — they're investments, not weaknesses.
- Use the question: "What is the most important thing I could be doing right now?"
- Learn to say: "I'm flattered, but I can't" — without explanation or apology.

## Controversy/Criticism

Critics argue that essentialism can become an excuse for inaction — saying no to everything avoids risk but also avoids opportunity. Others find the advice vague or impractical for people without control over their schedules (workers with multiple jobs, caregivers).

## One-Line Takeaway

> The disciplined pursuit of less — not getting more done in less time, but getting only the right things done, with ruthless elimination of everything else.
