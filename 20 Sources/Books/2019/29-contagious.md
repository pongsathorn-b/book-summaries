# Contagious — Jonah Berger

## Overview

Wharton marketing professor Jonah Berger spent years studying why things catch on. What makes content shareable? His research identified six core principles — the STEPPS — that explain why some products, ideas, and behaviors spread like epidemics while others die quietly. The book is a research-backed guide to making your ideas more shareable.

## Core Concepts

### Social Currency
We share things that make us look good to others. "Did you know…" and "I just tried…" are social currency — they signal knowledge, taste, or status. The more socially valuable the information, the more likely we are to share it. Find the remarkable — the counterintuitive, surprising, novel.

### Triggers
The environment activates memories and behaviors. Top of mind, tip of tongue. Some products are triggered by everyday cues: Godiva chocolate at Valentine's Day, beer at the Super Bowl. Effective triggers are frequent in the environment, not just powerful in the moment. Create the trigger that keeps your product top of mind.

### Emotion
High-arousal emotions spread — awe, excitement, amusement, anger, anxiety. Low-arousal emotions — sadness — don't. The key is physiological arousal: the body's state of activation. Use emotion to drive sharing, but ensure it's the right emotion for your message.

### Public
If it's built to show, it's built to grow. Products and behaviors that are publicly visible become models for imitation. Make the private public: the Nike swoosh, the Starbucks cup, the iPhone earbuds. Public visibility creates social proof and normative pressure.

### Practical Value
We share things that help others. The "cake mix" discovery — adding an egg to the boxed mix made it feel homemade and increased sales. People share useful information that saves time, money, or health — but the utility must be visible and specific.

### Stories
Stories are vessels for carrying information. We remember gossip more than advertising. The best brands embed their message in a larger narrative: the "Jerry Seinfeld's umbrella" story that makes State Farm memorable. Build your idea into a story worth retelling.

## Key Lessons

1. Social currency: share things that make people look good — find the remarkable.
2. Triggers: create environmental cues that keep your product top of mind.
3. High-arousal emotions spread — use awe, excitement, anger, not sadness.
4. Make the private public — products and behaviors that are visible become models.
5. Practical value: share useful information that helps others — be specific and visible.
6. Stories: embed your idea in a story people want to retell.
7. Virality is not magic — it's science. The six STEPPS are reproducible.
8. Not all sharing is good — ensure the story you're creating is the story you want.

## Practical Applications

- Ask "will people look smart/special/interesting when they share this?" before creating content.
- Identify and amplify environmental triggers — what cues your product in daily life?
- Design for public visibility — make your brand or behavior visible.
- Create practical value and make it shareable — "5 tips to…" outperforms "here's why…"
- Build your message into a story — don't just share facts, share narratives.
- Use high-arousal emotion: awe, excitement, even anxiety — not low-arousal emotions.

## Controversy/Criticism

Critics note that "viral" content is inherently unpredictable — the STEPPS improve odds but don't guarantee virality. Others argue the book is marketing-focused and may not apply to all types of ideas or behaviors.

## One-Line Takeaway

> Why things catch on follows six principles — Social Currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical Value, Stories — understanding them lets you engineer shareability.
