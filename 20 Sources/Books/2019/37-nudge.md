# Nudge — Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein

## Overview

Nobel-winning economist Richard Thaler and legal scholar Cass Sunstein's manifesto for "libertarian paternalism" — helping people make better decisions without restricting their freedom. The core insight: the way choices are presented (choice architecture) shapes decisions. Smart defaults, appropriate feedback, and understanding human psychology can nudge people toward better outcomes without eliminating choice.

## Core Concepts

### Choice Architecture
The way options are presented shapes which option people choose. This is unavoidable — someone always designs the choice environment. The question is whether that design is intentional or accidental. Smart choice architecture nudges people toward better decisions without forcing them.

### Smart Defaults
Most people accept defaults — they don't change the preselected option. This is true for retirement savings, organ donation, energy settings, and almost everything else. Making the healthier, wiser option the default — with the freedom to choose otherwise — is the simplest and most powerful nudge.

### The Three Major Biases That Nudges Address

1. **Present bias**: we value immediate rewards more than future benefits. This is why retirement savings is hard — the reward is 30 years away. Present bias is why "save more tomorrow" (commit to future increases) works better than "save more today."
2. **Status quo bias**: we prefer things as they are. Change is harder than staying the same. This is why defaults are so powerful.
3. **Loss aversion**: we feel losses more acutely than equivalent gains. Reframing a choice as avoiding a loss is more motivating than framing it as gaining the same amount.

### Econs vs. Humans
Classical economics assumes "Econs" — perfectly rational agents. Thaler and Sunstein argue for "Humans" — the actual species we are, with all our biases and shortcuts. Good policy designs for Humans, not Econs.

### The Four Principles of Nudge Design

1. **Incentives**: understand what really motivates people.
2. **Mapping**: understand how people understand the choice.
3. **Defaults**: make the desired option the default.
4. **Feedback**: give clear, timely feedback on choices.

### The "Better Choices" Box
For complex decisions (retirement, healthcare), create a "better choices" framework that simplifies the decision without eliminating options. This isn't simplification through removal — it's simplification through smart architecture.

## Key Lessons

1. Choice architecture is unavoidable — someone always designs how choices are presented.
2. Smart defaults are the most powerful nudge — most people don't change them.
3. Present bias explains why future rewards (retirement, exercise) are hard.
4. Humans are not Econs — good policy designs for actual human psychology.
5. Incentives, mapping, defaults, and feedback — the four elements of effective nudges.
6. Loss aversion can be used to motivate — reframing as avoidance increases motivation.
7. Nudges preserve freedom — the goal is better decisions, not forced decisions.
8. Test nudges empirically — what works in theory often doesn't work in practice.

## Practical Applications

- Audit defaults: are your desired outcomes the default options?
- Apply the 4 principles: incentives, mapping, defaults, feedback.
- Use "save more tomorrow" — commitment to future increases, not present sacrifice.
- Give feedback immediately — delayed feedback doesn't change behavior.
- Make the healthy option the default — with freedom to choose otherwise.
- Frame choices as avoiding loss, not gaining benefit — "you'll lose X if you don't…"
- Design for Humans: test your nudges with actual people before deploying.

## Controversy/Criticism

Critics argue "libertarian paternalism" is an oxymoron — paternalism is paternalism regardless of how it's framed. Others argue that nudges can be manipulative — exploiting biases without people's awareness. The book can feel too optimistic about the ability of "choice architects" to improve decisions.

## One-Line Takeaway

> The way choices are presented shapes decisions — smart defaults, incentives, and feedback can nudge people toward better outcomes without restricting freedom.
