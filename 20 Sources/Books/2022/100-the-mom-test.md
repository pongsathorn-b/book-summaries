# The Mom Test — Rob Fitzpatrick

## Overview

*The Mom Test* is the definitive guide to customer development for early-stage founders, named after the uncomfortable truth that your mom will tell you your idea is great regardless of its merit. The book's central insight: the way most founders gather feedback is useless because people are polite. They'll tell you they love your idea, that they'd totally use it, that they'd pay for it — and then never do. The Mom Test is a framework for asking questions that reveal genuine interest and real behavior, even when it means hearing things that hurt.

## Core Concepts

### The Mom Test Principle
The Mom Test: ask people about their lives, observe what they actually do, and ignore what they say. Real feedback comes not from "would you use this?" but from understanding what people actually spend time and money on, what they've tried before, and what they did last week. The goal is to extract information about real behavior, not opinions about hypothetical products.

### What Makes a Good Question
Good questions are about the customer's world — their behavior, what they've tried, what they've actually done with their time and money. Bad questions are about your idea — what they think of your feature set, whether they'd buy your product, whether the name is good. Asking about your idea invites polite lies.

### Three Simple Rules
**(1) Ask about their life, not about your idea** — "Tell me about the last time you did X" reveals real behavior. "Would you use a product that did Y?" invites polite fiction. **(2) Ask about specifics in the past, not vague plans for the future** — "What did you do last week?" is more revealing than "How do you envision managing this in the future?" **(3) Stay curious about the bad parts** — When people start complaining, listen hard. Unprompted problems are gold. Productive complaints reveal genuine pain.

### Finding Conversation Topics
Good conversation topics: what they currently do to solve the problem, what they've tried before, how they discovered solutions, what they wish existed, what they spend money on, and what they do with their time. Bad topics: your product, your features, your pricing, your vision.

### Running Good Customer Interviews
Fitzpatrick gives a precise framework: (1) Don't pitch — you're there to learn, not to sell. (2) Have a specific learning goal — what you want to know before you start. (3) Watch for bad news — painful truths are worth more than validation. (4) Avoid hypotheticals — "Would you pay $X?" is useless; observe what they've actually paid for. (5) Look for commitment signals — "That was interesting, send me more info" is not commitment; "Can I come back and see you Thursday?" is.

### The Five Types of Feedback
**(1) Fluff** — Polite interest. Ignore. **(2) Compliments** — "I love this idea." Meaningless. Thank them and move on. **(3) Hippo" (Highest Paid Person's Opinion)** — Unstructured guesses from people who aren't customers. Useless. **(4) Feature requests** — They want X. But do they actually pay for X? Observe behavior. **(5) Commitment and engagement** — They gave you their time, introduced you to others, pre-ordered, or did something that cost them something. This is the only type that matters.

## Key Lessons

1. **The Mom Test applies before you build anything.** Validate before you write a line of code.
2. **People will lie to be polite.** Your job is to ask questions where the truth is revealed regardless.
3. **Ask about the past, not the future.** What did you actually do last month? What have you actually tried?
4. **Bad news is more valuable than good news.** Painful truths save you months of wasted effort.
5. **Complements and "I love this idea" are noise.** Watch for real behavior instead.
6. **Feature requests reveal what people think they want, not what they actually need.**
7. **Commitment signals matter more than interest signals.** "This is interesting" is not commitment.
8. **Keep asking "why" until you hit the root of the problem.** Surface-level feedback is useless.
9. **Talk to at least 50 real users before building anything significant.**
10. **Your goal is to be wrong quickly and cheaply.** Learning fast beats being right.

## Practical Applications

- **Before building anything:** Conduct 50+ customer interviews using the Mom Test framework
- **Redesign your interview script:** Replace "Would you use X?" with "Tell me about the last time you had this problem"
- **Watch for commitment signals:** Name-dropping, introductions to decision-makers, pre-orders — these are the real validation
- **Look for unprompted complaints:** When someone starts complaining without you asking, dig deeper
- **Use screening questions:** "What did you do last week to solve this problem?" reveals real behavior before you pitch
- **Track learning outcomes:** Document each conversation and what you learned — look for patterns in what problems people actually pay to solve

## Controversy/Criticism

Critics argue the Mom Test is too extreme for some contexts — B2B enterprise sales and complex products may require different approaches. Some note that Fitzpatrick's advice to avoid pitching can be counterproductive when a pitch is exactly what a potential investor or partner needs to hear. The book's focus on early customer development may not apply to all startup stages.

## One-Line Takeaway

> Stop asking people if they like your idea — ask them about their lives and watch what they actually do; polite lies will kill your startup, but painful truths from real users will save it.
