# Thinking, Fast and Slow — Daniel Kahneman

## Overview

Nobel-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman maps the two systems that drive how we think. System 1 is fast, automatic, intuitive — the brain's autopilot. System 2 is slow, deliberate, effortful — the brain's analytical mode. Most of the time, System 1 runs the show, and System 2 endorses its quick judgments as rational. The result: we are confidently wrong about almost everything, and our confidence has nothing to do with accuracy.

## Core Concepts

### System 1 vs. System 2
System 1 operates automatically with little sense of voluntary control — detecting hostility, reading facial expressions, completing common phrases. System 2 allocates attention to effortful mental activities — complex calculations, comparisons, deliberate choices. System 1 never rests; System 2 is lazy by default and only engages when necessary.

### The Anchoring Effect
Arbitrary numbers change subsequent judgments. Ask people "was Gandhi older or younger than 144?" then "how old was Gandhi?" — they'll give a higher number than if you'd anchored at 35. Anchors are not just starting points — they pull estimates toward them and resist correction.

### The Availability Heuristic
We judge frequency by how easily examples come to mind. Plane crash coverage makes air travel seem more dangerous than driving — though it's far safer. The media's selection of what to cover shapes our perception of what is likely. Salient events are overweighted.

### Loss Aversion
Losses loom larger than equivalent gains — losing $100 feels worse than gaining $100 feels good. This is not rational (mathematically, they're equal) but psychologically universal. Loss aversion explains why people don't take jobs that pay more but have different titles, or why sunk costs drive bad decisions.

### The Planning Fallacy
We consistently underestimate the time, costs, and risks of future actions while overestimating our ability to execute. The solution: use the "outside view" — compare your plan to similar historical cases rather than your optimistic internal projection.

###WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is)
System 1 jumps to conclusions from limited information — and System 2 rubber-stamps these conclusions. We are overconfident because we never see the information we didn't consider. This explains why experts are often wrong and why we miss obvious alternatives.

## Key Lessons

1. System 1 runs the show — System 2 rubber-stamps its conclusions. Know which system is operating.
2. Anchors pull estimates toward them — always be aware of the first number presented.
3. Availability distorts frequency — media coverage shapes risk perception disproportionately.
4. Loss aversion makes us irrationally risk-averse — frame decisions to avoid triggering it unnecessarily.
5. The planning fallacy is universal — always use the outside view (comparative history) to estimate.
6. WYSIATI — what you don't see is as important as what you do. Seek disconfirming evidence.
7. Confidence ≠ accuracy — the most confident predictions are often the least reliable.
8. Slow down for important decisions — System 2 engagement is the antidote to System 1 bias.

## Practical Applications

- For important decisions, deliberately slow down — engage System 2 with explicit comparison and analysis.
- When negotiating, make the first offer — anchors have outsized effects on final outcomes.
- Use checklists and pre-mortems — force yourself to consider what could go wrong before committing.
- Practice "considering the opposite" — when confident, actively seek disconfirming evidence.
- Calibrate your confidence: track prediction accuracy over time to learn your actual reliability.
- Use outside view: compare your project to similar historical cases rather than your optimistic plan.

## Controversy/Criticism

Critics argue the two-system framework is overly simplified — the actual cognitive science is messier. Some behavioral economists argue Kahneman underweights System 2's role in creating System 1 biases. The book's academic origins show in density — it's readable but not light.

## One-Line Takeaway

> Your confident intuitions are mostly System 1 illusions — understanding the two-system model is the foundation of better judgment and the antidote to hundreds of cognitive biases.
