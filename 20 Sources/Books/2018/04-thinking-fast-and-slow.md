# Thinking, Fast and Slow — Daniel Kahneman

## Overview

Nobel Prize-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman reveals the two systems driving how we think. System 1 is fast, intuitive, emotional — it's what you use to catch a falling object or read a billboard. System 2 is slow, deliberate, logical — it's what you use to calculate 17×24 or fill out a tax form. Most of our decisions come from System 1 — and that's where the trouble starts. The book is the definitive account of cognitive bias and its implications for everything from investing to public policy.

## Core Concepts

### System 1: Fast Thinking
Automatic, intuitive, effortless. Makes snap judgments, sees patterns that aren't there. Prone to cognitive biases and logical errors. System 1 is always running — it's the brain's default. The problem: it never turns off, and it can't be switched off deliberately. System 1 is why you think you saw a ghost when you saw a coat on a chair in the dark.

### System 2: Slow Thinking
Deliberate, effortful, logical. Activated when System 1 hits its limits. Lazy — often accepts System 1's answers without checking. System 2 is what you engage when you catch System 1 making an error. But System 2 is also easily exhausted — ego depletion is real.

### Anchoring Bias
The first number you see anchors your judgment. In negotiations, the first offer sets the range. In restaurant pricing, the high-priced first item makes everything else seem reasonable. Anchoring is nearly impossible to resist — you know intellectually that the first number is arbitrary, but System 1 doesn't know.

### Availability Heuristic
Things easier to recall seem more common. Media influence on risk perception: plane crashes lead to fear of flying even though driving is far more dangerous. System 1 substitutes ease of retrieval for actual probability.

### Loss Aversion
Losses loom larger than equivalent gains. Losing $100 feels about twice as painful as gaining $100 feels good. This is why house money effects and sunk cost fallacies are so powerful — System 1 hates losses more than it loves gains.

### The Planning Fallacy
We consistently underestimate costs, risks, and time needed. The same person who accurately predicts their commute time will wildly overestimate their ability to complete a project. Optimism is the default — not because we're delusional, but because System 1 suppresses uncertainty.

## Key Lessons

1. System 1 is the author of most of our decisions — and it is not rational. Most "intuitions" are System 1 biases dressed up as wisdom.
2. You can never turn off System 1 — but you can learn to recognize when it's operating and engage System 2 to check it.
3. The planning fallacy is universal — always add buffer. Always.
4. Loss aversion means the fear of losing is more powerful than the hope of gaining. Use this in your own behavior design.
5. Anchoring is nearly impossible to avoid — protect yourself by making the first offer in negotiations.
6.WYSIATI: What You See Is All There Is. System 1 cannot imagine what it doesn't know — overconfidence is built into the architecture.
7. The conjunction fallacy: "Linda is a feminist bank teller" is more probable than "Linda is a feminist" — but System 1 will tell you the former is more likely.
8. Priming effects are real and invisible — what you were exposed to before a task changes your performance in ways you won't notice.
9. Narratives fool System 1 — a good story creates the illusion of understanding and predictability.
10. Calibration practice: how often do you say "I'm certain" and then turn out to be right? Most people are overconfident.

## Practical Applications

- In important decisions, always slow down deliberately. Ask: am I using System 1 here? What would System 2 say?
- Build in external review for major decisions — System 1 is blind to its own errors.
- Use checklists and algorithms for high-stakes decisions to bypass System 1's overconfidence.
- In negotiations, make the first offer to anchor — or be aware when others are anchoring you.
- Apply the "what would I think if this option weren't available?" question to escape the sunk cost fallacy.

## Controversy/Criticism

Some critics argue Kahneman overstates the case against intuition — in many domains (firefighting, chess, medicine), expert intuition is real and valuable. The book's academic density makes it less accessible to general audiences. Some psychologists argue the System 1/System 2 framework is a useful fiction rather than a literal description of brain architecture.

## One-Line Takeaway

> We're confident even when we're wrong — System 1 creates the feeling of knowing while System 2 lazily validates its conclusions.
