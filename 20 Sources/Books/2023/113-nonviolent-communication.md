# Nonviolent Communication — Marshall Rosenberg

## Overview

Marshall Rosenberg developed Nonviolent Communication (NVC) from decades of conflict mediation work in war zones and troubled communities. The core insight: much of human communication is designed to assign blame and punish, which generates defensiveness and escalation. NVC provides a four-step process for communicating that replaces judgment with observation, identifies feelings arising from unmet needs, makes requests free of demand, and receives others' needs with the same care. The result is communication that creates connection rather than conflict.

## Core Concepts

### The Four Steps of NVC

**1. Observation without evaluation** — Describe what you observed, as if watching through a camera, without judging whether it was good or bad. "You were on your phone for two hours after work" not "You always ignore me."

**2. Expressing feelings** — Identify and express how the observation made you feel. Use "I feel" followed by an emotion word (sad, anxious, frustrated, excited). Avoid "I feel that you..." which is actually a judgment.

**3. Connecting to needs** — Identify the human need that generated the feeling. "I feel lonely" (need for connection) or "I feel worried" (need for security). Needs are universal — everyone has the same fundamental needs.

**4. Making requests** — Make a specific, actionable request that addresses the unmet need. "Would you be willing to put your phone away for 30 minutes after work so we can talk?" The request is not a demand — it invites a response.

### The Difference between Feelings and Pseudo-Feelings
NVC distinguishes between genuine feelings and "pseudo-feelings" that are actually judgments dressed up as feelings. "I feel rejected" is a pseudo-feeling (it implies someone else rejected you); "I feel sad and disappointed" is a genuine feeling. The distinction matters because genuine feelings point to your own unmet needs; pseudo-feelings point outward and generate blame.

### Needs as Universal
Rosenberg identifies fundamental human needs — connection, autonomy, play, integrity, meaning, physical well-being — that underlie all human motivation. When needs are met, we experience positive feelings; when needs are unmet, we experience negative feelings. The violence in most communication comes from strategies to meet needs that disregard others' needs.

### Receiving with Empathy
NVC isn't just about expressing — it's equally about receiving. When someone shares something with us, we reflect back: **(1)** their observation, **(2)** their feelings, **(3)** their needs. We don't advise, explain, correct, or one-up. We just reflect understanding until they feel fully heard.

### The Role of Self-Empathy
Before connecting with others, NVC requires self-empathy: connecting with your own needs in the present moment. When you can honestly identify what you're feeling and needing, you can communicate without aggression or defensiveness. Self-empathy is the foundation for empathetic connection with others.

## Key Lessons

1. Most communication that generates conflict is judgment disguised as observation — "you always" and "you never" are signs you're evaluating, not observing.
2. Feelings point to needs — sadness signals an unmet need for connection; anger signals an unmet need for autonomy or fairness.
3. Needs are universal — everyone has the same fundamental needs for connection, security, meaning, autonomy.
4. Make requests, not demands — a request invites response; a demand triggers compliance or rebellion.
5. Pseudo-feelings ("I feel rejected") disguise judgments as emotions — use genuine emotion words.
6. Receiving with empathy requires reflecting the speaker's observation, feelings, and needs without advising or fixing.
7. Self-empathy — understanding your own feelings and needs — is the foundation for all nonviolent communication.
8. The goal is not to get what you want but to create connection that meets everyone's needs.
9. Behind every anger is an unmet need — finding the need transforms anger into productive dialogue.
10. NVC is a practice, not a technique — it requires lifelong cultivation of empathy and self-awareness.

## Practical Applications

- Replace "you always" and "you never" with specific, time-bound observations — "You've been on your phone three times since dinner"
- When upset, pause and identify: what am I feeling? What need of mine is unmet?
- Make requests instead of demands — "Would you be willing to...?" rather than "You need to..."
- Practice receiving with empathy — reflect back what you heard without advice or correction
- Use self-empathy daily — check in with your own needs before entering difficult conversations
- Replace "I feel that you..." with "I feel..." followed by genuine emotion words
- When someone shares a complaint, reflect their observation, feelings, and needs before responding

## Controversy/Criticism

Critics argue that NVC's non-judgmental stance can be used to avoid legitimate criticism or enable manipulative behavior. In high-stakes or time-sensitive situations, the full NVC process may be impractical. Some find the framework overly idealistic for genuinely adversarial relationships where one party is genuinely harmful. The book's emphasis on empathy can be emotionally demanding and may not suit everyone.

## One-Line Takeaway

> Nonviolent Communication replaces judgment with observation, connects feelings to universal needs, and makes requests rather than demands — creating connection even in the midst of conflict by focusing everyone on the same human needs.
