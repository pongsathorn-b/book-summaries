# Blue Ocean Strategy — W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne

## Overview

INSEAD professors Kim and Mauborgne's framework for creating new market space instead of competing in existing ones. The core insight: most companies compete in crowded markets — "red oceans" where competitors try to outperform each other, creating diminishing returns. The alternative: "blue oceans" — uncontested market space where competition is irrelevant. Blue ocean strategy is about creating demand, not fighting for existing demand.

## Core Concepts

### Red Ocean vs. Blue Ocean
Red oceans: existing market space, crowded with competitors, shrinking margins, "bloody" competition. Blue oceans: new market space, no competition, untapped demand. Most companies focus on red oceans — blue oceans are created by those who look beyond existing competition.

### Value Innovation
Blue ocean strategy is built on value innovation — simultaneously pursuing differentiation AND low cost. The key insight: value and cost trade off in red oceans (differentiate = higher cost). In blue oceans, innovation in value resets the cost frontier, allowing differentiation at low cost. Value innovation is the cornerstone of blue oceans.

### The Four Actions Framework
To create a blue ocean, ask four questions:
1. **Reduce**: Which factors should be eliminated well below the industry standard?
2. **Raise**: Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?
3. **Create**: Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?
4. **Eliminate**: Which factors, taken for granted by the industry, should be eliminated?

### The Six Paths
Kim and Mauborgne identify six paths to blue oceans:
1. **Look across alternative industries** — what do customers substitute this product with?
2. **Look across strategic groups** — what separates high-end and low-end buyers?
3. **Look across buyer groups** — who doesn't currently buy this category?
4. **Look across complementary product and service offerings** — what happens before/after use?
5. **Look across functional-emotional orientation** — what if you changed the functional/emotional appeal?
6. **Look across time** — how will trends reshape the industry?

### The Strategy Canvas
The strategy canvas graphs two dimensions: the factors the industry competes on, and the level of offering on each factor. A blue ocean is created by reconstructing factor curves in new ways. The canvas makes competition irrelevant by redefining the game.

### Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create Grid
Before creating a blue ocean, use the grid to identify which factors to eliminate, reduce, raise, and create. This structured approach prevents the common mistake of simply adding features (raising everything) without eliminating anything.

## Key Lessons

1. Competing in red oceans is bloody — create blue oceans of uncontested space.
2. Value innovation: simultaneously pursue differentiation AND low cost.
3. Use the Four Actions Framework: eliminate, reduce, raise, create.
4. Six paths to blue oceans: across alternatives, strategic groups, buyer groups, complements, functional-emotional appeal, and time.
5. The strategy canvas makes competition visible — and makes blue oceans visual.
6. Don't try to outcompete rivals — make competition irrelevant.
7. Create demand, don't compete for demand — the blue ocean creator defines the market.
8. Focus on the big picture, not the details — blue oceans require reconstructing factor curves.

## Practical Applications

- Use the Six Paths framework to identify blue ocean opportunities.
- Apply the ERRC grid (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create) to your industry.
- Draw your strategy canvas: what factors does your industry compete on?
- Look across alternatives: what do customers substitute your product with?
- Look across buyer groups: who doesn't currently buy, and why?
- Focus on functional-emotional: can you change the appeal from functional to emotional?
- Use the strategy canvas to align your entire organization around a blue ocean move.

## Controversy/Criticism

Critics argue that blue oceans are rare and difficult to sustain — once a blue ocean is identified, competitors flood in. Others find the framework too abstract — difficult to operationalize without significant consulting support. The book can feel like advocacy rather than balanced analysis.

## One-Line Takeaway

> Competing in crowded red oceans produces diminishing returns — the key to growth is creating blue oceans of uncontested market space through value innovation.
